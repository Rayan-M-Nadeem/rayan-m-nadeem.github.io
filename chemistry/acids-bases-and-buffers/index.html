Acids, bases & buffers

Acid–base behavior is about proton transfer and competing equilibria, not simply whether a liquid feels “strong.”

01 / The story

Strength is not concentration

A Brønsted acid donates a proton; a base accepts one. Strong acids dissociate essentially completely in water at ordinary concentrations, while weak acids establish equilibria. Strength describes the tendency to transfer a proton; concentration describes how much substance is present. A dilute strong acid and a concentrated weak acid can have very different pH values.

02 / The story

A logarithmic scale

pH encodes hydronium activity, often approximated by concentration in introductory problems. One pH unit corresponds to a tenfold change in hydronium concentration in that approximation. Conjugate acid–base pairs differ by one proton; acid and base equilibrium constants are linked through water’s ionization at a given temperature.

03 / The story

Buffers and titrations

A buffer combines a weak acid with its conjugate base, consuming modest added acid or base. It works best when both components are present in appreciable amounts and has finite capacity. A titration curve traces the changing solution; the pH at equivalence is not always 7, because conjugate species may react with water.

Where it shows upBuffers stabilize blood and laboratory reactions; acid–base analysis helps formulate medicines and monitor natural waters.
