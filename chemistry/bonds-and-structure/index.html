Bonds & structure

A chemical bond is an energy-favorable arrangement of electrons and nuclei, not a physical stick between atoms.

01 / The idea

From electrons to shapes

Lewis structures track valence electrons as bonds and lone pairs. Formal charge helps compare plausible arrangements, and resonance describes a single electron distribution that cannot be captured by only one drawing. Electron groups repel and establish approximate geometry; lone pairs can compress bond angles. A molecule’s shape and bond polarities together determine whether it has a net dipole.

02 / The idea

Several kinds of solid

Ionic solids contain extended arrays of oppositely charged ions; metals have mobile electrons; network-covalent solids contain continuous bonding; molecular solids consist of discrete molecules held together by intermolecular attractions. These structural differences explain why melting points, brittleness, and electrical conductivity differ. A formula like NaCl names a ratio in a crystal, not a separate NaCl molecule.

03 / The idea

How bonds explain properties

Bond order and bond length reflect how electron density is shared. Greater bond order often means a shorter, stronger bond, but comparing different elements requires more care. A substance’s bulk behavior also depends on attractions between its particles and on its structure, not merely on the strength of one bond.

04 / The idea

A model has a job

Lewis drawings, VSEPR, and orbital models answer different questions. A Lewis structure counts valence electrons; VSEPR predicts a rough arrangement around a central atom; orbital ideas explain how electron density can support that arrangement. None is a photograph of a molecule. Expanded-octet drawings and resonance should be treated as representations with limits rather than literal alternating bonds.

05 / The idea

From one bond to a whole material

An ionic lattice is stabilized by many attractions, which helps explain its high melting point. In a metal, mobile charge carriers account for conductivity even while atoms remain in a solid structure. A molecular substance may have strong covalent bonds within molecules yet melt easily because the attractions between molecules are weaker. Always name which interaction you are comparing.

Where it shows upMaterials scientists tailor ceramics, semiconductors, and polymers by controlling bonding and structure; molecular shape helps explain drug–target recognition.
The relationship, in wordsFormal charge = valence electrons − nonbonding electrons − ½(bonding electrons). It is bookkeeping, not a measured atomic charge.
Common mix-upA polar bond does not guarantee a polar molecule; symmetrical bond dipoles can cancel.
