Atoms & evidence

An atom is not a tiny solar system. Its identity is set by its protons, while its electrons determine most of its chemistry.

01 / The story

What the evidence tells us

Isotopes have the same proton count but different neutron counts, so they share an element name while differing in mass. Mass spectrometry separates charged particles by mass-to-charge ratio and reveals isotope abundances. A periodic-table atomic mass is a weighted average, not usually the mass of one atom. Light absorbed or emitted at particular wavelengths shows that electrons occupy quantized energy states.

02 / The story

Why the periodic table has a pattern

Electrons fill orbitals—regions described by probability, not little fixed tracks. Valence electrons are especially important because they participate in bonding. Across a period, increasing nuclear charge usually pulls electrons closer; down a group, additional shells and shielding tend to increase atomic size. These trends have exceptions, so the electron arrangement matters more than a slogan.

03 / The story

A useful way to read a formula

The mole links a count of particles to a measurable amount: one mole contains about 6.022 × 10²³ entities. Molar mass converts between grams and moles, and percent composition tells what fraction of a sample’s mass comes from each element. Empirical formulas give the smallest whole-number ratio; molecular formulas give an actual molecule’s atom counts.

Where it shows upMass spectrometers identify isotope mixtures in geochemistry and forensics; emission spectra help identify elements in a sample or star.
The relationship, in wordsAverage atomic mass = Σ(isotope mass × fractional abundance). Each isotope contributes in proportion to how common it is.
Common mix-upAtomic number is not atomic mass, and an orbital is not a circular path.
