Atoms & evidence

An atom is not a tiny solar system. Its identity is set by its protons, while its electrons determine most of its chemistry.

01 / The idea

What the evidence tells us

Isotopes have the same proton count but different neutron counts, so they share an element name while differing in mass. Mass spectrometry separates charged particles by mass-to-charge ratio and reveals isotope abundances. A periodic-table atomic mass is a weighted average, not usually the mass of one atom. Light absorbed or emitted at particular wavelengths shows that electrons occupy quantized energy states.

Changing the neutron count changes an isotope's mass and sometimes its stability, but it does not turn carbon into another element. The isotope abundance pattern of a sample can therefore act as a fingerprint. A mass spectrum does not simply print a chemical name: peaks must be connected to possible ions, isotope ratios, and the instrument's assumptions. In an elemental spectrum, neighboring peaks can correspond to different isotopes of the same element.

When an excited atom releases light, the color corresponds to an energy difference between allowed states. A continuous range of possible energies would not produce the same distinct lines. Scientists combine this light evidence with measurements of charge, mass, and scattering to build atomic models. Each method answers a different question; none directly displays a little atom with its electrons as a photograph might display a planet.

02 / The idea

Why the periodic table has a pattern

Electrons fill orbitals—regions described by probability, not little fixed tracks. Valence electrons are especially important because they participate in bonding. Across a period, increasing nuclear charge usually pulls electrons closer; down a group, additional shells and shielding tend to increase atomic size. These trends have exceptions, so the electron arrangement matters more than a slogan.

A period is a row in which valence orbitals are filled in a broadly predictable pattern. Elements in a group often react similarly because their outer electrons are arranged similarly, though size and available orbitals change down the group. For example, the alkali metals all tend to lose one valence electron, yet their reactions with water differ in vigor. That common behavior is a consequence of electron structure rather than merely a visual coincidence on a chart.

To explain a size trend, compare attraction and distance. Moving across a period usually adds protons while incoming electrons occupy the same general shell; shielding does not fully cancel the stronger pull. Moving down adds a shell, placing the outer electrons farther away and behind more inner electrons. Ions also change size: losing electrons can remove a shell or reduce repulsion, while gaining them often increases repulsion. Compare species with the same electron count to isolate nuclear-charge effects.

03 / The idea

A useful way to read a formula

The mole links a count of particles to a measurable amount: one mole contains about 6.022 × 10²³ entities. Molar mass converts between grams and moles, and percent composition tells what fraction of a sample’s mass comes from each element. Empirical formulas give the smallest whole-number ratio; molecular formulas give an actual molecule’s atom counts.

A mole is a counting unit, as a dozen is, but its enormous size makes microscopic particles weigh a laboratory-scale amount. If a formula represents ionic material, the counted entity is a formula unit rather than a discrete molecule. In a mixture, the grams of each component add, but mole counts follow the particles or formula units chosen. Naming the entity avoids common unit mistakes in calculations.

The empirical ratio need not identify a unique compound. CH₂O is the smallest ratio for formaldehyde, and other molecular formulas can share it. To find a molecular formula, you need the compound's molar mass in addition to composition. In experimental work, measured percentages may not land on perfect whole numbers; rounding, impurities, and uncertainty must be considered before multiplying ratios into integers.

04 / The idea

Reading electron evidence

Photoelectron spectroscopy measures the energy needed to remove electrons from a substance. Peaks reveal groups of electrons with different binding energies, while relative peak areas help show how many occupy a subshell. A jump in successive ionization energies can suggest when removal has crossed from valence electrons into a more tightly held core. These observations support electron arrangements; they do not let us watch an electron travel in an orbit.

In photoelectron spectroscopy, higher binding energy means an electron was more difficult to remove. The peak area reflects how many electrons contribute, while peak position reflects their binding. A spectrum of a multi-electron atom therefore reveals structure within what a single Bohr-style shell picture hides. When interpreting a graph, first check which direction the binding-energy axis runs; some presentations place high values on the left.

Successive ionization energies tell a related story. Removing the first electron leaves a more positive ion, so removing another generally takes more energy. A particularly large jump can indicate that the valence electrons have been exhausted and a core electron is next. The size and location of that jump help infer valence count, but the exact energies are shaped by subshells and electron repulsion. This is evidence for an arrangement, not a universal shortcut that replaces it.

05 / The idea

Trends with reasons, not arrows

Ionization energy is the energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion. Electron affinity concerns adding one; the two are related but not opposites with identical magnitudes. Effective nuclear charge and electron shielding explain much of periodic behavior. Subshell energies and electron repulsions create meaningful exceptions, so compare actual electron configurations when a trend appears to break.

Atomic radius is not a hard surface with an edge. Different operational definitions, such as covalent radius or ionic radius, can yield different numbers. The trend is still useful when comparisons use the same definition. Ionization energy similarly depends on a specified process: the first ionization removes one electron from a neutral gaseous atom, while later ionizations start from ions. Comparing unlike processes without saying so obscures the reasoning.

Electron affinity can be especially tricky. Adding an electron may release energy, but an atom's subshell occupancy can make the process less favorable than a simple left-to-right trend predicts. Noble gases and filled or half-filled subshells illustrate why “closer to fluorine” is not a complete explanation. When a measured value surprises you, draw or describe the electron configuration and ask about attraction, shielding, and repulsion before declaring an exception arbitrary.
