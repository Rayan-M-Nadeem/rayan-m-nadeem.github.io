The First World War

A European crisis widened into a global war fought by empires, soldiers, and civilians, reshaping borders and the meaning of political power.

01 / The story

Why a crisis became a war

The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 triggered a crisis, but it did not by itself make a world war inevitable. Austria-Hungary sought to punish Serbia; Germany supported its ally; Russia mobilized; France and Britain made decisions under pressure. Alliances, military timetables, nationalist politics, and imperial rivalry narrowed the room for compromise.

Historians continue to debate the relative responsibility of governments. The crucial question is how individual decisions during July turned a regional conflict into one crossing borders. A chain of declarations is the result of choices, not a mechanical law of alliances.

The immediate crisis unfolded through ultimatums, mobilizations, and diplomatic messages. Decision-makers had incomplete information about what other governments would accept and about the consequences of their own plans. Nationalist organizations and imperial competition formed the background, but they did not dictate each telegram or declaration. Studying the July Crisis day by day shows the difference between a long-term pressure and a short-term decision.

02 / The story

The first campaigns

Germany’s invasion of Belgium brought Britain into the European war. Campaigns in 1914 did not produce the quick victories many leaders expected. On the Western Front, armies eventually dug extensive defensive systems across France and Belgium. In the east, fronts moved more, with enormous losses and shifting territory.

A line on a map can make a battle seem abstract. It also meant displaced families, destroyed farms, soldiers traveling far from home, and towns trying to supply or escape armies. The opening months already showed that fighting would reach civilians as well as soldiers.

03 / The story

What trenches required

A trench was part of a system of communication lines, dugouts, artillery positions, roads, and supply depots. Soldiers rotated through positions rather than living permanently in one narrow ditch. Mud, weather, lice, fear, and shellfire shaped daily life. Even quiet sectors required carrying ammunition, repairing defenses, and watching for attack.

The scale of artillery and machine-gun fire made crossing exposed ground dangerous. Commanders experimented with coordination, surprise, and new tools, but learning took lives. A photograph of one trench can reveal conditions without representing every front or season.

Letters from soldiers can be intimate and vivid, but censorship and a desire not to alarm family shaped what they wrote. A unit diary recorded movements and losses in a different official voice. Archaeology can reveal trench layouts and objects after the landscape has changed. Reading these sources together helps recover routine tasks as well as rare attacks, and it prevents the most dramatic image from standing for four years of experience.

04 / The story

Attrition and 1916

Verdun and the Somme became symbols of prolonged battles in 1916. Leaders sought to exhaust opponents, break positions, or relieve allies, while soldiers and nearby communities endured staggering losses. The Somme’s first day was disastrous for British forces, yet the campaign continued for months and included changing tactics.

It is easy to dismiss every participant as blindly repeating the same plan. Studying operational decisions and their limits does not justify the toll; it explains why armies persisted and how technology, intelligence, weather, and politics constrained them.

05 / The story

The war beyond western Europe

Fighting reached the Ottoman Empire, the Caucasus, Africa, the Middle East, and the seas. Colonial troops and laborers served across theaters, often without equal pay, status, or later recognition. The 1915 Gallipoli campaign and campaigns in Mesopotamia illustrate strategic goals and local consequences far from the trenches in France.

Calling the conflict a world war requires following people drawn into it by imperial rule. A soldier from India, a carrier in East Africa, and a resident of a besieged city did not experience one common European story.

Colonial contributions included military service and enormous amounts of labor, food, and material. Indian troops fought in several theaters; African carriers sustained campaigns under dangerous conditions. Recognition in later national commemorations has been uneven. To describe the war as global, follow the routes through which people were recruited or compelled, the terms of their service, and their claims after returning home.

06 / The story

The Armenian genocide

During the war, Ottoman authorities deported and murdered Armenians on a massive scale beginning in 1915. War and security claims were used to justify policies that targeted a population. Survivors, witnesses, diplomatic records, and later research document the genocide.

It must not be reduced to a regrettable byproduct of battle. The decisions of officials and the actions of perpetrators mattered. Including the genocide in the war’s history also shows that civilian suffering included deliberate persecution, not only incidental exposure to combat.

07 / The story

Sea power and blockade

Navies protected transport, fought for control of seas, and tried to interrupt an opponent’s supplies. Britain’s blockade restricted goods reaching the Central Powers; German submarines attacked shipping, including civilian vessels. Each strategy affected food, trade, diplomacy, and neutral countries.

A naval map is a map of households and markets too. The loss of imports could change diets and prices, while a ship sinking could influence public opinion across an ocean. War at sea was never only a contest between fleets.

08 / The story

The home front

Governments expanded control over industry, transport, food, and information. Women worked in new or enlarged roles in factories, farms, hospitals, and offices, while still carrying household burdens. Wages, rationing, inflation, and grief varied by class and place. Propaganda encouraged sacrifice and could suppress criticism.

A poster inviting people to serve tells us what officials wanted, not necessarily how everyone responded. Letters and local records reveal enthusiasm, reluctance, fatigue, and disagreement within the same society.

Governments also policed dissent. Laws on secrecy, conscription, and speech differed by country and changed over time. Some people supported war aims while objecting to a particular measure; others opposed the conflict on religious or political grounds. The home front contained argument alongside sacrifice. Studying a strike, a food queue, or a local newspaper can reveal tensions a patriotic poster was designed to smooth over.

09 / The story

Medicine and new weapons

Medical services treated wounds, infections, and psychological trauma on a huge scale. Gas weapons caused terror and lasting injury, though artillery remained a major source of casualties. Tanks and aircraft appeared in expanding roles; neither instantly solved the problem of defended lines.

Innovation came through dangerous trial, error, and large logistical systems. A new device is only one part of a battlefield; training, repair, fuel, communications, and evacuation determine how it changes people’s chances of survival.

10 / The story

Revolution and Russia’s exit

The Russian Empire faced military losses, food shortages, and a crisis of legitimacy. Revolutions in 1917 overthrew the monarchy and then brought Bolsheviks to power. The new government withdrew from the war through the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk in 1918, surrendering vast territory.

Russia’s experience was not just a move on the Western Front’s chessboard. Revolution, civil conflict, peasant demands, and national movements reshaped a huge region. Other governments reacted to those changes with hope or alarm.

11 / The story

The United States enters

The United States joined the war in 1917 amid unrestricted submarine warfare, diplomatic tensions, and arguments over its role. American troops arrived in growing numbers in 1918, adding manpower and resources to the Allies. US participation did not erase the years of fighting and sacrifice by other states and their colonies.

At home, mobilization expanded federal power and generated disputes about speech, loyalty, race, and labor. A nation’s public ideal of democracy must be compared with who enjoyed equal rights while it fought.

12 / The story

The armistice and the fighting’s end

The German offensives of spring 1918 failed to secure victory; Allied counteroffensives and strain within the Central Powers led to collapse. The armistice of 11 November stopped fighting on the Western Front. It did not resolve every border, civil conflict, or human displacement.

An armistice is a military agreement to halt fire, not a complete peace. For prisoners, refugees, and grieving families, return and rebuilding took far longer. Other conflicts continued in parts of the former empires.

13 / The story

Peace settlements and mandates

The Treaty of Versailles in 1919 imposed terms on Germany; other treaties addressed the collapsed Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman empires. The League of Nations was created to promote international cooperation. New boundaries and mandate arrangements often reflected the priorities of victorious powers over local self-determination.

It is too simple to say one treaty alone caused the next war. Economic crises, ideologies, and later choices were also crucial. But the settlements left grievances and unresolved questions with major consequences.

Promises of self-determination were applied selectively. Arab political movements, Armenian survivors, and colonized communities encountered settlements made by powers with strategic interests. The mandate system presented external rule as temporary stewardship while limiting local sovereignty. A peace conference can therefore be read through what its delegates wrote and through the petitions of people whose futures were being decided without equal representation.

14 / The story

Counting and remembering

Casualty figures differ by whether they count military deaths, civilian deaths, disease, and later effects. Memorials name some people and leave others out. Diaries, photographs, official reports, and oral histories preserve distinct viewpoints; censorship and retrospective memory complicate them.

A battlefield image can make a war seem universal even though much fighting occurred elsewhere. Compare the record of a unit with the record of a community that supplied it, and ask whose loss received a monument.

15 / The story

What changed after 1918

Four imperial orders fell or were transformed, and political movements sought new states and rights. The war accelerated social change without automatically granting equality. Veterans faced injury and reintegration; colonized people pressed promises of self-determination that imperial leaders often denied.

The war remains a turning point because its effects crossed generations, not because history moved directly from one catastrophe to another. Understanding it requires both major decisions and the lives those decisions rearranged.

The influenza pandemic of 1918–1919 overlapped the war’s end and affected soldiers and civilians worldwide. It did not arise as a simple military weapon or obey the armistice date. Demobilization, travel, and public-health decisions shaped its spread. Including disease in the aftermath changes how we picture a society “returning to normal”: many families faced another wave of loss just as they expected peace.

The longer viewThe war’s consequences ran through destroyed communities, collapsing empires, contested peace settlements, and demands for political change.
