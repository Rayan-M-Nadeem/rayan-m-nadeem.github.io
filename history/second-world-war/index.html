The Second World War

A global war of conquest, occupation, genocide, and mass mobilization remade political boundaries and civilian life across continents.

01 / The story

The road to war

The post-1918 settlement, economic depression, authoritarian movements, and decisions by governments all shaped the 1930s. Nazi Germany pursued rearmament and territorial expansion under a racist ideology; Fascist Italy and imperial Japan also pursued conquest. Other powers’ responses included negotiation, sanctions, and efforts to avoid another war.

The invasion of Poland by Germany on 1 September 1939 began the European war. Japan’s war against China had already begun in 1937, with roots in earlier imperial expansion. A global chronology cannot start and end with Europe alone.

The 1930s contained multiple crises rather than one straight path from Versailles to 1939. The Japanese invasion of Manchuria in 1931, Italy’s invasion of Ethiopia in 1935, the Spanish Civil War, and Nazi expansion tested international responses. Each had its own causes and participants. The weakness of collective security matters, but so do the aggressive choices of regimes that sought territory and racial domination.

02 / The story

Conquest in Europe

German forces defeated Poland and then several western European states in 1940. Britain continued to resist; Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941. These campaigns relied on combined arms, logistics, and political decisions, not a magical new tactic. Occupation changed daily life through requisition, censorship, forced labor, and persecution.

The map of rapid victories can hide the human cost in each occupied town. People faced choices under coercion: some collaborated, some resisted, and many tried to survive. Motives and opportunities differed by place.

03 / The story

The war in Asia and the Pacific

Japan’s imperial expansion imposed harsh occupation across East and Southeast Asia. The attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941 brought the United States into war, but people in China and elsewhere had already endured years of fighting. Naval and air campaigns stretched across islands and oceans.

A battle such as Midway mattered strategically, yet civilian suffering under occupation, forced labor, hunger, and bombardment must remain in the account. Asia was a central theater with its own political histories, not an appendix to Europe.

China’s war against Japan caused immense suffering over many years, including the 1937 massacre in Nanjing. Chinese political and military factions both fought and competed, while civilians faced occupation, displacement, and shortages. A chronology that begins the Pacific war at Pearl Harbor overlooks these earlier years. The same applies to people in Southeast Asia whose experiences of Japanese occupation and Allied campaigns differed sharply from an American island-hopping narrative.

04 / The story

The Holocaust

Nazi Germany and its collaborators systematically murdered six million Jews. Persecution grew through exclusion, confiscation, ghettos, mass shootings, deportation, and killing centers. Roma and Sinti, disabled people, Soviet prisoners of war, Polish civilians, and others were also targeted by Nazi policies in different ways.

The Holocaust was not an accidental consequence of wartime chaos. Ideology, institutions, individual perpetrators, and opportunities created by conquest shaped it. Survivor testimony, documents, sites, and material evidence must be read with care and respect.

The murder of European Jews developed through policies that changed as Nazi Germany expanded. Mass shootings in occupied eastern Europe and killing centers were central. The record includes perpetrator documents, photographs, material sites, and survivor testimony. No one source is sufficient, but the evidence is overwhelming. The subject needs precise language: a concentration camp, a forced-labor site, and a killing center had different functions, even when a person’s suffering crossed those categories.

05 / The story

Occupation and resistance

Underground networks passed intelligence, hid people, printed forbidden materials, or attacked occupiers. Some resistance grew from national, religious, or leftist politics; it was rarely a single unified movement. Helping a persecuted neighbor carried enormous risk. Many people had little ability to make a public choice.

Postwar stories often simplify society into heroes and collaborators. Local records show a harder landscape of coercion, survival, opportunism, and courage. Naming that complexity does not reduce responsibility for persecution.

06 / The story

The Eastern Front

The German invasion of the Soviet Union produced immense battles and civilian devastation. The siege of Leningrad, fighting at Stalingrad, and the later Soviet offensives involved extraordinary loss. Nazi plans for conquest and racial domination made the war in the east especially brutal.

The Soviet Union’s role in defeating Nazi Germany was decisive, and its population paid a staggering price. That fact does not erase Stalinist repression or the experiences of people caught between regimes. A balanced account can hold several realities together.

07 / The story

North Africa and the Mediterranean

Campaigns across North Africa involved British and Commonwealth, German, Italian, and other forces, with local populations facing disruption. Control of sea routes, ports, and the Suez region mattered. Allied landings and subsequent fighting in Italy opened further fronts.

Desert battles are often told as duels between generals, yet fuel, shipping, colonial labor, and the lives of residents determined what armies could do. The theater connects European war to imperial geography.

08 / The story

Civilian mobilization

Factories, farms, transportation, and households were reorganized for war. Women took on expanded work in many countries, though changes in opportunity and pay were uneven. Rationing, evacuation, and state messaging altered daily routines. People in occupied and unoccupied areas faced very different forms of pressure.

A home-front poster is evidence of a government’s appeal, not proof everyone agreed. Compare it with wages, letters, labor disputes, and the lives of workers excluded from its ideal image.

09 / The story

Bombing and cities

Air campaigns hit factories, transport, and urban neighborhoods. London, Hamburg, Dresden, Tokyo, and many other cities experienced destruction and civilian deaths. Leaders argued that bombing could weaken industry or morale; historians debate its military effects and moral implications.

Aerial photographs show damage at a scale that can erase individual homes. Survivor accounts and local records restore the people who lived beneath the target map. Technology increased a state’s reach without resolving responsibility for how it was used.

Bombing raises hard questions about intended targets and foreseeable civilian harm. Governments had different capabilities and strategies, and the military impact of campaigns remains a subject of study. That debate must begin with the people killed and displaced, not only with tonnage or an aerial view. Diaries, municipal records, and testimony can connect strategic calculations to life on the ground.

10 / The story

The Atlantic and supply

Allied forces depended on ships carrying food, troops, fuel, and equipment. German submarines tried to sever these routes; escorts, intelligence, aircraft, and industrial shipbuilding helped counter them. The outcome affected whether armies could be supplied and populations fed.

A convoy was a system of workers, ports, sailors, and decisions. It illustrates that victory depended on production and transport as well as battlefield success.

11 / The story

Turning points are plural

By 1942–1943 the Axis faced setbacks at Stalingrad, El Alamein, and Midway, among other campaigns. No single battle “won” the global war. Different coalitions fought in different theaters, and each victory required maintaining resources and political commitment.

The Allied invasion of Normandy in June 1944 opened a major western front in occupied France, while Soviet armies advanced from the east. Their operations were connected but not interchangeable. Each also affected civilians in its path.

12 / The story

War crimes and responsibility

Occupation policies, genocide, massacres, forced labor, and mistreatment of prisoners require specific evidence and attribution. Responsibility can lie with governments, commanders, institutions, and individuals. Not every civilian in a state made the same choice, and that fact does not excuse a regime’s crimes.

Postwar trials at Nuremberg and elsewhere sought legal accountability, with limits and uneven reach. The surviving archive is vast, yet historians still test documents, testimony, and physical sites against each other.

13 / The story

The war ends in 1945

Germany surrendered in May 1945. In the Pacific, Allied advances, Soviet entry into the war against Japan, and the US atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki preceded Japan’s surrender in August and formal signing in September. Historians debate the relative influence of these events and the decisions that produced them.

The atomic bombings killed and injured large numbers of civilians and opened a nuclear age. Describing the end as a clean moment of relief obscures survivors, prisoners, refugees, and people still returning home.

Hiroshima and Nagasaki were inhabited cities. The bombs caused immediate blast and heat injuries and later radiation effects; survivors, called hibakusha, preserved testimony that a military map cannot convey. The Soviet attack on Japanese forces in Manchuria also changed the strategic situation. Historians disagree on counterfactual claims about surrender, so an honest account states the evidence and the debate without presenting any civilian death as an abstraction.

14 / The story

A world of displaced people

The war left cities ruined and millions displaced. Survivors of Nazi persecution sought family, homes, and safety; many found they could not return. Borders shifted and populations were expelled or moved. The United Nations was founded in 1945, and reconstruction took different forms under growing US-Soviet rivalry.

European empires faced intensified demands for independence in Asia and Africa. The postwar order combined new institutions and aspirations with enduring inequality and fresh conflict.

15 / The story

Reading the war through lives

Official campaign maps explain movements; testimony explains experience; records of persecution reveal policy and its implementation. None should be used in isolation. A leader’s speech cannot stand for a whole society, and a photograph needs a date, place, and provenance.

The Second World War is too large for one national story. Follow a decision across a factory, a front, an occupied neighborhood, and a displaced family. That method preserves scale without losing the people who lived it.

War memory differs across countries and communities. A liberation story can coexist with a survivor’s account of displacement or a colonized person’s demand for independence. Museums and memorials select objects and names for specific purposes. Comparing those choices with archives and testimony is part of historical work. It lets us honor resistance and sacrifice while still asking whose suffering a familiar national narrative has overlooked.

The longer viewVictory ended the Axis regimes but left millions dead, displaced, and facing the new questions of reconstruction, justice, and decolonization.
